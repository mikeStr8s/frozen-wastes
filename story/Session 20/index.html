Session 20: The White and the Wanderer

The party struck out from Termalaine into the open wilderness, the dogsled cutting fresh tracks through unmarked snow. The Sea of Moving Ice lay somewhere ahead — how far, exactly, was a question the landscape declined to answer. They traveled through the full length of the day until the dogs began to labor and the light bled out of the sky, then made camp in the lee of a low ridge and settled in for a cold night.

Yin Shen took first watch.

The camp was quiet. The wind pressed low across the tundra and the dogs curled into one another for warmth. Then Dzaan lurched upright from his bedroll with the sudden, breathless energy of a man surfacing from drowning.

He was already moving before Yin Shen could speak. His hands swept through the snow in long deliberate strokes, carving lines and angles into the frozen ground — a teleportation circle, drawn freehand and fast, the geometry of it precise in a way that suggested the pattern lived somewhere deep in his hands rather than in his waking mind.

His words came in a tangle. Red Wizards of Thay. Here in Ten-Towns. Looking for him. He could not say how they had found him or how long he had before they did, only that if they found him alongside the party, the party became a problem they would also need to solve. He would not allow that.

Yin Shen asked about the magic training he had promised her.

He stopped. For a moment the frantic energy went still. Then, without hesitation, he reached into his coat and produced a small folio — handwritten, tight script filling every margin — and pressed it into her hands.

"Keep practicing," he said. He tried to smile. It almost worked.

Arcane light gathered between his palms. The circle blazed blue-white against the snow, and then he was gone. The lines faded. The snow settled back into the shape of the tundra as though nothing had been drawn there at all.

When the others woke and the disappearance was discussed, the party was quiet for a moment. Then they broke camp, and Aronious took watch while the rest settled back in.

He was leaning against a rock, scanning the dark, when something jabbed him sharply in the ribs.

He looked down. A Chwinga stood at his side — tiny, bright-eyed, trembling with barely contained delight at its own audacity. When Aronious did not immediately shout or swat at it, the creature dissolved into a fit of tinkling laughter.

He played along. They sat together in the cold for a while, the ranger and the small elemental spirit, occupying the same patch of dark in the way that two very different creatures sometimes find themselves briefly sharing the same world. Eventually the Chwinga darted behind the rock. It reappeared a moment later carrying a snowflake — enormous relative to its small frame, pristine and many-pointed, glimmering faintly with something that was not quite ordinary light.

With great ceremony, it pressed the charm into Aronious's hand.

Then it turned, retrieved two more charms from the ground behind the rock, and set them carefully where Hobbins and Yin Shen would find them in the morning. It looked back at Aronious once — a look that seemed to say that was entirely the plan all along — and disappeared.

By the time the dogs were rested and the party was moving again, the landscape had changed. The ground grew harder, the air sharper, and the horizon began to show the grey-white suggestion of pack ice. The Sea of Moving Ice announced itself gradually and then all at once — a frozen expanse broken by pressure ridges and deep blue fissures, stretching away into a sky that seemed to press down rather than lift.

The shipwreck was not difficult to find. Its hull rose from the ice at a list, half-consumed by the freeze, every timber grey and salt-stripped. The hull had been opened — not by rot, not by weather, but by something that had simply decided a wall was an inconvenience. The edges of the breach were smooth in a way that suggested size and indifference in equal measure.

From inside came a rhythmic, bestial hammering.

The party exchanged a look and went in anyway.

The frost troll filled the hold the way a storm fills a valley — completely, and with a sense that it had been there longer than anything else. It came for them without preamble, talons and teeth leading, its pale bulk terrifyingly fast in the confined dark.

What followed was close and brutal. Aronious put arrows into it from every angle he could find. Yin Shen moved through the shadows between its strikes with the economy of someone who understood that a single clean hit mattered more than volume. Hobbins hurled spells with reckless precision, reading the creature's regeneration and understanding, with a wizard's cold clarity, that cold would not save them here.

She switched to fire.

The troll's flesh stopped knitting. Its movements grew ragged. And when the moment arrived, the Shield Guardian settled the matter with characteristic efficiency — punching straight through the rotten hull as though the timber were parchment, then punching the troll so hard it left the ground entirely and met the far wall at considerable speed. The fire Hobbins had already set in its flesh did the rest. It did not get up.

The hold creaked and settled around them. Beneath the ice, the dragon's hoard glimmered. The party began stacking rotten wood to build a fire, hoping to melt enough of the freeze to reach it.

While the fire took hold, Yin Shen explored the hold's deeper compartments. A barricaded door at the far end had clearly been what the troll was hammering against. She opened it.

Four kobolds stared back at her.

They were disheveled but intact, and — notably — they did not attack. The party asked about the dragon. The kobolds confirmed they served it. The party asked if the dragon was friendly toward Ten-Towns.

The kobolds found this so genuinely funny that two of them had to sit down.

The party began to deliberate. Hobbins sent periodic fire bolts skyward — makeshift flares punching bright orange against the grey — while Aronious did the same with flaming arrows. They waited.

The dragon arrived the way weather arrives: not all at once, but with a building pressure that preceded it by some margin. Thunderous wingbeats. The crack of ice giving way. A shadow moving across the ship's hull that was larger than the hull itself.

Then a voice — low, resonant, and terribly pleased with itself.

"Ahh. I smell the tender flesh and hear the panicked heartbeat of the meal that so eagerly delivered itself to me."

The kobolds immediately burst into laughter and fled into the darkest corners they could find.

The party pressed into the shadows. As the dragon's great serpentine head pushed through the breach, they saw its eyes — filmed over, pale and clouded. The creature was ancient enough that its sight had largely failed it. It was hunting by smell and sound.

Then a voice from outside, calm and almost conversational: "I think you could use some help."

An incantation. A fog cloud rolled through the hold in a single dense surge, swallowing everything. "To me!"

They ran toward the voice.

They emerged from the ship into the open ice to find a figure standing in the fog — nine feet tall, broad as a doorway, wrapped in walrus pelts, with a club and shield already being shouldered into carry position. She did not wait for introductions.

"Quick. My cave."

They ran. Behind them the dragon's howl split the air with a fury that was equal parts hunger and insult, and it answered its own anger with a blast of breath aimed at the sky. Spears of ice came down from above like thrown lances, slamming into the frozen ground in a tight and terrible rain.

One landed close enough to Hobbins to throw her forward off her feet. Yin Shen caught her without breaking stride and kept moving. Aronious went down twice on the broken ice, and twice Yin Shen found him, pulled him upright, and shoved him forward.

They hit the cave entrance at a run and kept going until the stone closed around them and the sound of the dragon faded to a distant, furious echo.

The giant set her club and shield against the cave wall with the unhurried manner of someone returning from an unremarkable errand. As she straightened, she passed close to a low rock shelf, and a small shape emerged from the shadow behind it — a Chwinga, its face marked with a pattern that felt immediately, inexplicably familiar. It reached up. The giant extended her little finger, and the creature touched it gently.

A greeting. Old and certain, between two beings who had known each other for some time.

The party stood in the cave mouth, breathing hard, and said nothing for a moment. Outside, ice continued to fall.
